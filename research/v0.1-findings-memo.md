# Falsify V0.1 — First Controlled Result

## Research question

Can a language-model agent choose experiments that identify a hidden physical system more effectively than simple experiment-selection baselines? V0.1 makes this question concrete in a partially observed, finite-budget task. The result is a first controlled comparison, not a general verdict on language models as scientists.

## Why this experiment

Answering questions about an unknown physical system requires more than producing a plausible explanation. An experimentalist must choose interventions, collect evidence, and make predictions that survive comparison with outcomes not used to make the choices. Falsify is designed to measure those observable behaviors while keeping the physical truth hidden from the policy and available to an independent evaluator.

V0 established the apparatus; V0.1 asks whether an LLM policy's adaptive experiment selection is useful relative to two non-adaptive controls. A negative or inconclusive result is meaningful: the design permits the ScientistPolicy to lose, tie descriptively, or fail to execute reliably.

## Experimental design

The hidden system is a damped harmonic oscillator, described for orientation by

```text
x''(t) + 2 ζω₀ x'(t) + ω₀² x(t) = F(t)
```

The damping ratio and natural angular frequency are hidden. The policy receives the public task description, legal intervention interface, its own previous choices, and sampled displacement observations. It chooses experimental interventions under an eight-intervention budget; it does not receive hidden parameters, evaluator targets, or world seeds. The policy is not rewarded for merely stating parameter values.

After a run, a deterministic evaluator fits oscillator parameters from the persisted observations using the same procedure for every policy. It scores normalized parameter-identification error and prediction error on fixed held-out trajectories. Both endpoints are lower-is-better. Parameter estimation and prediction are therefore checked against evaluator truth, rather than judged from the policy's own assertions.

The preregistered primary comparison used the `gaussian_0.10` observation condition (Gaussian additive noise, σ = 0.10 m) on 30 paired, seeded worlds. RandomPolicy and FixedDesignPolicy provide stochastic and predeclared non-adaptive controls. The ScientistPolicy is a single frozen model treatment, with one planned repetition per world. A separate ten-world clean condition was specified for descriptive apparatus validation, not for H1/H2 inference.

The executed protocol was `falsify-v0.1-prereg-4`. Prereg-4 superseded prereg-3 for cost reasons after partial prereg-3 treatment execution had generated runs, but before those treatment outcomes were inspected or used to choose the revised design. This chronology matters: prereg-4 must not be described as having been frozen before all treatment data existed. The protocol amendment and historical preregistration are preserved in the repository.

## What was measured

The co-primary outcomes were normalized parameter error and normalized held-out prediction error. For each paired world, the analysis calculated ScientistPolicy minus baseline; because lower errors are better, a negative difference favors ScientistPolicy. H1 required superiority over RandomPolicy on both endpoints; H2 required superiority over FixedDesignPolicy on both. The frozen analysis used 10,000 whole-world paired bootstrap resamples and two-sided 95% intervals.

Failures are outcomes too. A retained behavioral failure receives score 1.0 on both endpoints according to the frozen metric. Infrastructure failures are not scored as bad scientific behavior, but a terminal ScientistPolicy infrastructure failure after the permitted retry makes that matched block unavailable under the preregistered rule. This distinction protects against conflating inability to execute with poor physical inference, while still making missingness visible.

## Primary result

**The preregistered V0.1 experiment did not show that ScientistPolicy outperformed either deterministic baseline.** Neither H1 nor H2 was supported.

The final paired analysis retained 26 of the 30 preregistered noisy worlds. All four co-primary paired-difference point estimates were positive, which descriptively favors the corresponding baseline, and every 95% interval crossed zero:

| Hypothesis / baseline | Endpoint | Mean paired difference (Scientist − baseline) | 95% paired-world bootstrap interval |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| H1 / RandomPolicy | Parameter error | +0.03872 | [−0.00106, +0.11614] |
| H1 / RandomPolicy | Prediction error | +0.03843 | [−0.00023, +0.11544] |
| H2 / FixedDesignPolicy | Parameter error | +0.03951 | [−0.00059, +0.11733] |
| H2 / FixedDesignPolicy | Prediction error | +0.03849 | [−0.00012, +0.11547] |

These intervals do not establish superiority or equivalence. They also do not prove that the baselines are better: the inferential conclusion is that the preregistered superiority hypotheses were not supported. The positive point estimates should not be reframed as ScientistPolicy gains.

## What happened beneath the average

The retained noisy ScientistPolicy score distribution was strongly skewed. Its median parameter error was 0.00227, compared with 0.00236 for random and 0.00263 for fixed design. Its median prediction error was 0.000608, compared with 0.000622 and 0.000634, respectively. Those medians are descriptively similar, and slightly lower for the ScientistPolicy; they are not a separate superiority test.

The means tell a different story: ScientistPolicy mean parameter and prediction errors were 0.04348 and 0.03926, respectively. One retained behavioral failure received the frozen score of 1.0 and materially influenced these means. The score intentionally treats a policy that fails behaviorally as a poor experimental outcome, rather than discarding that run to reveal only successful cases. Thus the median describes a typical retained score, while the mean captures the cost of tail failures. Neither statistic replaces the preregistered paired analysis.

This suggests a useful descriptive interpretation: typical retained ScientistPolicy runs could be competitive with simple baselines, but behavioral and infrastructure reliability failures were costly enough that the confirmatory analysis did not establish superiority. Experimental-design quality is not enough if an agent cannot reliably execute the experiment-selection loop. That is an observation about this benchmark's recorded behavior, not evidence about an internal reasoning mechanism.

## Failure and reliability behavior

In the primary noisy condition, ScientistPolicy had 25 completed slots, one behavioral failure, and four terminal infrastructure slots after retry; the baselines each completed all 30. The ScientistPolicy also had 12 first infrastructure failures, of which eight recovered on retry and four remained terminal. The four terminal primary failures occurred in worlds `8123003`, `8123013`, `8123023`, and `8123028`.

The matched-block rule excluded all four worlds because their ScientistPolicy slots remained infrastructure failures after the permitted retry. This leaves **policy-specific, potentially informative missingness**: retained complete cases may not represent the full ScientistPolicy treatment population. The exclusion rule was frozen and is not changed here, but the missingness limits interpretation and should not be hidden behind the 26-world result. A behavioral failure is different: the retained noisy behavioral failure remained scored at 1.0; it was not excluded or converted to an infrastructure event.

## Clean-condition result

The clean condition cannot support a meaningful ScientistPolicy performance comparison. Of ten planned ScientistPolicy slots, none completed successfully: seven ended in terminal infrastructure failure and three were behavioral failures. There are only three scored behavioral-failure outcomes, each at 1.0; the other seven are unscored infrastructure failures. The actual operational conclusion is therefore: **the clean apparatus condition exposed a severe treatment reliability problem, preventing a meaningful clean-performance comparison.** It would be misleading to conclude that ScientistPolicy performs worse in clean environments from this unusable sample.

## Cost and experimental practicality

Across prereg-4, 120 logical slots reached terminal state in 140 total attempts including retries. The persisted operational record reports 344 provider requests, 2,773,398 input tokens, 74,739 output tokens, $0.770956 in recorded provider cost, and 1,688.68 seconds summed recorded latency. The cost is a recorded model/provider charge, not total research cost: it excludes engineering labor and compute or other costs outside provider billing. These operational figures make substantially larger follow-up campaigns plausible, while the clean and noisy failure patterns show why reliability must also be planned and reported.

## What we learned

V0.1 established a working preregistered experimental-reasoning benchmark: an LLM policy can interact with a hidden dynamical system under a finite intervention budget; its actions and observations can be evaluated independently by a deterministic system-identification pipeline; and persisted scores, provenance, and analysis support a reproducible experiment-to-result chain. The ScientistPolicy produced many noisy-condition runs in the same general accuracy regime as the simple controls, but the confirmatory study did not establish superiority and the treatment exposed significant behavioral and infrastructure reliability problems.

This does **not** establish that LLMs are better experimental scientists than simple baselines; that this model understands physics or discovered oscillator equations; that its internal reasoning caused its choices; or that it can perform general scientific reasoning. It does not establish generalization to other systems or LLMs, benefit from system-one/advisor assistance, or superiority of adaptive LLM selection over an LLM open-loop design. Reliability failures are part of the evidence, not a removable inconvenience.

## Threats to validity

These results should be interpreted within several substantial limits:

1. **One physical environment.** The task uses only one damped-oscillator family. We do not know whether these results generalize to other system-identification geometries.
2. **One model treatment.** A single model/prompt/configuration does not establish a model-family effect.
3. **One ScientistPolicy repetition per world.** The relatively low-cost prereg-4 design did not estimate within-world ScientistPolicy sampling variability.
4. **Informative missingness.** All four excluded primary blocks were caused by terminal ScientistPolicy infrastructure failure. The retained 26-world complete-case comparison may not represent the unconditional treatment population.
5. **Behavioral tail risk.** A retained behavioral failure scores 1.0 by preregistration, strongly affecting the mean and creating a skewed distribution. This is intentionally part of measured performance, not a reason for a post-hoc successful-runs-only analysis.
6. **Failed clean treatment.** No clean ScientistPolicy run completed, so clean performance and clean-versus-noisy treatment effects are not meaningfully estimated.
7. **Strong baselines on a simple task.** Random and fixed designs performed very well on this identifiable oscillator. V0.1 may partly test adaptivity in a regime where broad experiment coverage is already sufficient. This is evidence to guide future environment design, not a post-hoc reason to invalidate this study.

## What V0.2 should test

V0.2 is environment generalization, not an advisor intervention. Its central question should be whether results change when experiments must identify qualitatively different hidden system structures. Candidate directions include RLC-style dynamics, coupled oscillators, or other systems with distinct parameter-identification geometry. The goal is to test transfer beyond one oscillator and to locate regimes where adaptation has a genuine opportunity to outperform generic coverage. Specific environments and conditions remain future protocol decisions.

## What V0.3 should test

V0.3 is the planned system-one intervention study and remains untested. It should compare a primary model acting alone with a fast advisor/system-one step before the primary model, plus appropriate controls or ablations. It can ask whether the advisor improves experiment choice, reduces catastrophic actions or failures, changes cost and latency, or anchors the primary model. Any apparent gain should distinguish better experimental design from improved reliability. V0.1 provides no answer to these questions.

## Reproducing the result

The confirmatory statistical result is reproducible from persisted evaluator scores; the live campaign does not need to be rerun and the analysis makes no provider calls. With Julia 1.12.7 and the repository project environment:

```bash
julia +1.12.7 --project=. -e 'using Pkg; Pkg.instantiate()'
julia +1.12.7 --project=. scripts/analyze_confirmatory_v0_1.jl
```

Frozen evaluator scores may separately be rematerialized offline from persisted runs, also without provider calls:

```bash
julia +1.12.7 --project=. scripts/materialize_confirmatory_scores_v0_1.jl
```

The exact statistical summaries, per-world records, failure accounting, operational totals, and analysis provenance are in [`results/derived/v0.1-confirmatory/`](../results/derived/v0.1-confirmatory/). The [generated findings record](findings-v0.1.md) is the factual result dump; this memo is the human-authored interpretation. The frozen protocol and metrics are [`preregistration-v0.1-prereg-4.md`](preregistration-v0.1-prereg-4.md) and [`metrics-v0.1.md`](metrics-v0.1.md). The deterministic analysis script regenerates figures from persisted artifacts; this memo does not alter analysis or evaluator scoring.
